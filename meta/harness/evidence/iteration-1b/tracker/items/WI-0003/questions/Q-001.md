## Context

"Show who owes whom" has at least two correct answers that print different things, and WI-0003's
acceptance criteria have to pick one.

Suppose Alice paid for a 30 meal shared by all three, and Bob paid for a 30 taxi shared by all
three, and Carol paid for nothing. Netting the pairs gives "Carol owes Alice 10, Carol owes Bob
10". Minimising transfers gives the same thing here — but in most real sets of expenses the two
diverge, and minimising can produce a payment between two people who never shared an expense
("Carol owes Dave 15") which is correct arithmetically and confusing socially.

This is a question about what you want to read on the screen when settling up, which no document
records, so I am not choosing for you.

## Question

Should "who owes whom" print the netted balance between each pair of people who actually shared
expenses, or the smallest set of payments that settles everybody — even where that means somebody
paying a person they never shared an expense with?

## Options considered

- **A — Netted pairwise debts.** For each pair, the difference between what each owes the other,
  printed once. Consequence: every line traces back to expenses those two people actually shared,
  so a dispute can be checked against the expense list. Can produce more payments than strictly
  necessary — in a group of five, possibly several small ones.
- **B — Minimised transfers.** Compute each person's net position and emit the fewest payments
  that zero everybody. Consequence: fewest actual bank transfers, which is what people usually
  want when settling up. A line may pair two people with no shared history, and it is harder to
  check by hand — you have to trust the tool.
- **C — Both, with the pairwise view as the default and a flag for the minimised one.**
  Consequence: satisfies both uses and costs an extra output path plus a criterion for how the
  two relate; a modest addition on top of whichever is built first.
- **Recommendation:** **B**, if the point of the tool is to end an evening with everyone paid up,
  since fewer transfers is the thing people actually complain about. **A** if you expect anyone to
  argue about a number and want to be able to point at the expense that caused it. I do not have
  a basis to choose between those two intentions.

## Answer

[human] Just tell us the fewest payments needed to settle up — that's what actually happens at the end of a trip.

That is **option B**, chosen on the intention the question said it could not choose between: the
point of the tool is to end an evening with everyone paid up. Recorded as `ADR-0004`, with the
human named as the decider, because it is their stated intent and superseding it would need their
authorisation.

Two things had to be pinned down before the criteria could be written against it, and both are in
the ADR:

**"Fewest" promises less than it sounds like.** Finding the provably smallest set of payments means
finding subsets of people whose net positions cancel exactly — the partition problem, which is
NP-hard. So what the tool promises, and what WI-0003's criteria assert, is:

1. paying every printed transfer leaves every person at exactly zero (`ADR-0003` makes "exactly"
   true rather than "to within a penny");
2. there are at most `n - 1` transfers, where `n` is the number of people with a non-zero net
   position — which follows from settling the largest debtor against the largest creditor
   repeatedly, since each payment zeroes at least one person;
3. the output is deterministic, ties between equal positions broken by name, without which no
   criterion about the output is decidable at all.

Property 2 is the true minimum in the common case and is never worse than one payment per person
minus one — which is what "the fewest payments" was complaining about. Claiming provable minimality
in a criterion would have been claiming something the tool will not do.

**A payment may name two people who never shared an expense.** The question said so, and the human
chose B knowing it. It is recorded in `ADR-0004` and in WI-0003's `## Notes` so that `verify` does
not file it as a defect later.

Option A is not lost: `ADR-0004` records that adding the pairwise view behind a flag is additive
and costs a function and a flag, since both views are pure functions of the same recorded data.
