## Context

Most real splits do not divide evenly. Three people sharing a 10.00 bill owe 3.333… each; two
decimal places cannot represent that, so somebody has to be a penny out. This is not a detail
that can be deferred to implementation: it changes what the acceptance criteria on WI-0002 and
WI-0003 can even assert, because "the amounts printed, if paid, leave every person at zero"
(WI-0003 AC4) is either true by construction or false by a penny, depending on the rule chosen
here.

There is nothing in the record to answer it from, and any choice is defensible, which is exactly
the case the question protocol says to bring to you rather than to pick silently. I am asking now
rather than at implementation time because the rule has to appear in the criteria.

## Question

When an expense does not divide evenly among its sharers, how should the leftover pennies be
assigned?

## Options considered

- **A — Give the remainder to the payer.** Three people, 10.00: the payer's share is 3.34, the
  others 3.33. Consequence: totals always reconcile exactly, the rule is one sentence, and the
  person who is out of pocket by a penny is the one who already fronted the money. Slightly
  arbitrary but never surprising.
- **B — Spread the remainder over sharers in a fixed order.** Pennies go to the first sharers by
  name order until exhausted. Consequence: also reconciles exactly and is fair over many
  expenses if the order rotates — but a fixed order is not fair, it just always penalises whoever
  is alphabetically first.
- **C — Keep exact fractions internally, round only when printing.** Consequence: balances are
  mathematically exact and no rule is needed for entry. But the printed numbers then do not sum
  to the total, so WI-0003 AC4 becomes "within a penny" — the tool tells you to pay amounts that
  do not quite settle the debt.
- **Recommendation:** **A.** It keeps every printed figure exactly reconcilable, which is the
  property that makes the output trustworthy for actually handing over money, and it is the
  easiest rule to explain to the group when someone notices the extra penny.

## Answer

"Go ahead anyway" is an instruction to proceed, so this is decided rather than escalated again.
"We'll decide later" is a constraint on how: the choice has to stay cheap to change.

**Decision: option A — the odd pennies go to the payer**, recorded as `ADR-0003`, together with the
representation the rule needs to mean anything.

1. Money is a whole number of minor units, and all arithmetic on it is integer arithmetic. Binary
   floating point is not used for money anywhere in the tool: `0.1 + 0.2 != 0.3` would make the
   reconciliation guarantee false for reasons unrelated to the rounding rule.
2. An amount is entered with at most two decimal places — `12`, `12.5` and `12.50` all mean 1250
   minor units — and more precision than that is refused rather than silently rounded. Amounts are
   always printed with two decimal places.
3. To divide `R` among `k` sharers, each gets `R // k` and the first `R % k` of them get one penny
   more, in this order: the payer first if the payer is one of them, then the rest in the order
   they were named.
4. The shares of an expense therefore sum to its total exactly, always.
5. **Shares are derived on every run, never stored.** What is recorded is the expense as entered.

Point 5 is the part that answers "we'll decide later". Because no derived share is persisted,
superseding this rule with a different one changes no recorded data: it changes one function, and
the next run prints the new answer for every expense the group ever entered. The group can change
its mind at any time and lose nothing. Point 1 is the exception, and the ADR says so — the integer
representation is what every exactness guarantee rests on and is not meant to be cheap to reverse.

Option C was rejected because it makes `WI-0003 AC4` false by a penny per person: the tool would
print amounts that do not quite settle the debt, which is the one property that has to hold if
anybody is going to hand over money on the strength of the output. Option B was rejected because a
*fixed* order is not fair — it always penalises whoever sorts first — and making it fair needs
rotation, which needs stored state, which contradicts point 5.
