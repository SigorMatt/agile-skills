## Context

This is refinement for WI-0004, deleting a card, round 1, question 2 of 2. The other one asks
how you say which card you mean. That is all of them for this item.

Deleting is the only thing this tool will do that cannot be taken back. Everything else is
additive or repeatable: a card you add can sit there unused, a card you grade wrong comes back
tomorrow, and a deck file that goes bad is refused rather than overwritten. A deleted card is
gone — this item's `## Out of scope` rules out an undo, a trash can and any recovery, and we are
not proposing to change that, because a tool that keeps everything you deleted is a tool that
never really deletes anything.

That puts it next to the one sentence you were most emphatic about, in `EP-001/Q-001`:
*"don't lose my progress — that's the one thing that would make this a failure."* You said it
about the deck surviving between sessions, and the tool takes it seriously in the places you
meant: writes go through a temporary file and a rename so an interrupted `add` cannot truncate
your deck, and a deck file it cannot parse is refused loudly rather than replaced with a fresh
empty one. Removing a card on purpose is a different thing from losing one by accident — but
removing the *wrong* card is not, and it is the one way this tool could ever lose you something
you wanted.

So this is a question about how much the tool gets in your way at that moment, and it is yours
rather than ours because the cost of both answers falls entirely on you: either a prompt you did
not want, every time, or a card you did not mean to delete, once.

## Question

When you delete a card, should `recall` show you the card and ask you to confirm before removing
it, or just remove it?

## Options considered

- **A — just remove it.** `recall delete <the card>` takes the card out, prints both its sides so
  you can see what went, and exits. Consequence: one command, no keystrokes you did not choose to
  type, and deleting three cards is three commands with nothing in between. It is the shortest
  path and the one closest to *"nothing fancier than that"*. The cost is that if you name the
  wrong card — a typo that happens to match another card, or the right question on the wrong one
  of two similar cards — it is gone before you have seen it, and getting it back means typing it
  in again from memory.
- **B — show it and ask.** The command prints the card it is about to remove, both sides, and
  asks `delete this card? [y/n]`; it removes the card on `y` and leaves the deck untouched on
  anything else. Consequence: you see the card before it goes, so naming the wrong one costs you
  a keypress instead of a card, and the only irreversible thing the tool does takes a deliberate
  yes. It is the same shape of prompt a review sitting already uses, so it is nothing new to
  learn. The cost is one extra keypress on every deletion, including the ones you were completely
  sure about.
- **C — ask by default, with a way to skip.** As B, but `recall delete --yes <the card>` removes
  it without asking. Consequence: safe when you are half-attending, out of the way when you are
  clearing out a batch. The cost is a flag to remember, and the flag is the one that switches the
  guard off — which is where the accident B was protecting you from comes back, usually at the
  moment you have got into the habit of typing it.
- **Recommendation:** ours is **B**, and it is a preference rather than a finding. Deleting is
  rare compared with adding and reviewing, so the prompt costs you a keypress a few times a
  month; and a card you deleted by mistake is the only way we can see for this tool to lose you
  something, which is the failure you named. If you would rather it just did what you said
  without checking, **A** is a perfectly good answer and we will build it without a guard and say
  so plainly in the documentation. **C** is a real answer too, and it is the one we would pick if
  you expect to delete cards in batches.

## Answer

[human] B — show me the card and ask first. I'd rather have one extra keypress than lose a card
to a typo.
