## Context

Split 10.00 three ways and each share is 3.333…, which is not an amount anybody can pay. Some
rule has to decide where the missing penny goes, and whichever rule it is, WI-0003's AC3 says the
printed amounts must net to zero to the last minor unit — so the rounding cannot be left to
whatever the arithmetic happens to do.

The choice is small in size and large in consequence: it is the difference between a report that
balances exactly every time and one that is off by a penny occasionally, which is precisely the
kind of thing that destroys trust in a tool about money even though the sum involved is
meaningless.

Definition of Ready criteria this blocks: **R4** and **R10** for AC3 — an uneven division is one
of this item's modes and its behaviour is unspecified.

## Question

When an amount does not divide evenly among the people sharing it, who absorbs the remainder —
and should the tool ever print a fraction of a penny?

## Options considered

- **A — The payer absorbs the remainder; everything is printed to two decimal places.** 10.00
  split three ways charges the two non-payers 3.33 each and leaves 3.34 with the person who
  paid. Consequence: totals balance exactly, the rule is one sentence you can explain at the
  table, and the person who was already out of pocket carries a penny more. Over many expenses
  it is roughly self-cancelling because the payer changes.
- **B — The remainder is spread deterministically among the sharers, in name order.** The first
  sharers alphabetically each take an extra penny until the total is exact. Consequence: also
  balances exactly, and is fairer than A in a group where one person always pays. The cost is a
  rule that is harder to explain and that makes Alice a penny worse off forever.
- **C — Keep exact fractions internally, round only when printing.** Consequence: internal
  arithmetic is exact, but two printed figures can then fail to sum to a third, which is exactly
  what AC3 forbids. Named so the option is on the record; I do not recommend it.
- **Recommendation:** **A**. It is the rule most tools of this kind use, it is explainable in one
  sentence, and it puts the odd penny on the person who chose to pay rather than on whoever is
  first in the alphabet. If your group has one person who always pays, **B** is the fairer
  choice and I will record it instead.

## Answer

[human] Not sure yet — go ahead anyway, we'll decide later.

**[answer-questions]** *"Not sure yet — go ahead anyway, we'll decide later."* is a delegation
with a deadline attached, and both halves are honoured: the decision is made now so nothing is
blocked, and it is made in the form that keeps "later" genuinely available.

Recorded as **option A — the payer absorbs the remainder; every figure prints to exactly two
decimal places**, as `docs/architecture/adr/ADR-0004-payer-absorbs-the-rounding-remainder.md`.

This could not simply be left open. AC3 requires the printed amounts to net to zero *to the last
minor unit*, and `EP-001` carries the same as a success measure; `10.00` shared by three people
cannot be split into three equal printable figures, so somebody's must differ by a penny and the
only open question is whose. Option C — exact fractions internally, rounded at print time — is not
available at all, because it is precisely the case where two printed figures fail to sum to a
third, which AC3 forbids. So the real choice was A or B, and A is what the question recommended:
one sentence you can say at a table, and the odd penny falls on the person who chose to pay rather
than on whoever is first in the alphabet.

**Why deciding this was safe on "we'll decide later".** The remainder rule is applied when the
report is computed and is never persisted — balances are recomputed from the stored expenses on
every run. Switching to option B later is one function, this ADR, and AC6; there is no migration
and no data to reinterpret. Had the rule been baked into stored data, the honest response to
"we'll decide later" would have been to escalate instead, because "later" would already have
passed.

The ADR also fixes the consequence that makes AC3 true by construction rather than by luck: money
is held and computed as **integer minor units**, never as a binary float. `12.10` has no exact
float, and `WI-0002/Q-003` has already established that the tool must never silently alter the
user's number. A tool that refuses to round its input and then prints figures that do not add up
would fail `EP-001/Q-005`'s *"nobody argues about it"* by a different route.
