## Context

"Show who owes whom" is the whole point of the tool, and it is the one phrase in your idea that
has three reasonable readings. They are not cosmetic variations: they print different numbers of
different things, and only one of them is what you would read out in a pub.

Take four friends and a few expenses where Alice paid for dinner, Bob paid for the taxi and
Carol paid for nothing. The three readings give:

- *pairwise*: Bob owes Alice 12, Carol owes Alice 12, Alice owes Bob 6, Carol owes Bob 6 —
  every debt each expense created, four lines, and Alice and Bob owe each other simultaneously;
- *net per person*: Alice +18, Bob −6, Carol −12 — what each person is up or down overall, three
  lines, but nobody is told who to pay;
- *settlement*: Carol pays Alice 12, Bob pays Alice 6 — the smallest set of payments that clears
  everyone, two lines, and each line is an instruction someone can act on.

WI-0003's AC2 (the Alice/Bob/Carol worked example) happens to read the same under all three, so
it does not disambiguate. I am not choosing this for you, because it is what the output *is*.

Definition of Ready criteria this blocks: **R4** for AC1 and AC2 — "a statement of who owes whom"
is not decidable until this is settled.

## Question

What should the report print: every pairwise debt as it arose, each person's net position, or
the smallest set of payments that settles the group?

## Options considered

- **A — Settlement: the smallest set of payments that clears everyone.** Consequence: shortest
  output, every line is an action, and it is what a group actually needs at the end of a trip.
  The cost is that the payments do not correspond to any particular expense — Carol may be told
  to pay Alice for a taxi Bob paid for — so it can look wrong to someone who was expecting to see
  their own dinner in it.
- **B — Net per person.** Consequence: the simplest thing to compute and to check by hand, and
  it makes AC3 (the amounts net to zero) obvious by construction. The cost is that it does not
  actually answer "who owes *whom*" — it answers "how is everyone doing".
- **C — Pairwise debts, unnetted.** Consequence: fully traceable; you can point at any line and
  find the expense behind it. The cost is that it grows with the number of expenses rather than
  the number of people, and it will tell two friends to pay each other on the same evening.
- **D — Settlement, with net-per-person shown alongside.** Consequence: A's actionable list plus
  B's checkable summary; the two together also let a sceptic verify the tool. Costs a slightly
  longer output and a second thing to specify.
- **Recommendation:** **D**, with **A** as the fallback if you want it terse. The reason for
  showing both is EP-001/Q-005: a settlement list on its own is the kind of output somebody
  disputes and nobody can check, whereas the net line next to it is checkable against your own
  memory of what you paid. If you tell me the group would rather have four short lines than
  seven, I will record **A**.

## Answer

[human] I want the actual payments — who pays whom — not just a list of who's up and down. A
quick per-person summary alongside it is fine too if that's easy, but the payments are what
matters.

**[answer-questions]** Recorded as **option D — the settlement, with net positions alongside**.
"I want the actual payments — who pays whom" chooses A over B and C; "a quick per-person summary
alongside it is fine too" adds B's summary to it, which is D. `EP-001/Q-005` independently
confirms both halves — *"one command tells us who pays whom and nobody argues about it"* — and
that answer is the reason the summary is treated as required rather than as the optional extra
the phrasing "if that's easy" might suggest: the net line is the only thing in the output a
sceptic at the table can check against their own memory, and "nobody argues" is not otherwise
observable.

**One thing in this question could not be delivered as worded, and the item now says so.** The
question offered "the **smallest** set of payments that settles the group". Minimising the number
of transfers that clears a set of balances is NP-hard; a tool that claimed it would either be
wrong or would hang. Since an acceptance criterion has to be decidable by someone with a terminal,
`WI-0003` states what the tool actually does — net positions, then repeatedly match the largest
debtor with the largest creditor, ties broken by name — and claims only what follows from it: at
most `k − 1` payments for `k` people with a non-zero net, and identical output on a re-run. The
algorithm and the reason for not attempting minimisation are
`docs/architecture/adr/ADR-0005-settlement-by-greedy-largest-first-matching.md`. In a friend group
the greedy result differs from the true minimum by at most a payment or two, and often not at all.

The same ADR carries the constraint that `EP-001/Q-002` left behind: computing net positions is a
separate stage from turning them into payments, so that a repayment can later be netted in as one
more transfer without the settlement code changing. That promise was made when settling up was
deferred, and it lives on the item now rather than only in the epic's question file.
