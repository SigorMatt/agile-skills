## Context

This is intake for **EP-001**, the dice roller, round 1 — the second of three questions.

`3d6+2` is the only expression you gave us, and it is a sample rather than a specification: it
is written "something like `3d6+2`". How far "something like" reaches is the single biggest
driver of how much work this is, because each step below roughly doubles the size of the thing
that reads the expression. It also decides what `WI-0001` has to accept, and its acceptance
criteria cannot be finished without it.

We would rather ask than pick, because the cheap answer and the generous answer are both entirely
reasonable and we cannot tell from here which game you play.

## Question

Which dice expressions must the tool accept?

## Options considered

- **A — One dice term with an optional modifier.** `3d6`, `d20`, `2d10+5`, `4d8-2`.
  Consequence: the smallest useful tool; covers most single rolls in most systems. Anything more
  complicated has to be typed as two separate rolls and added up by hand.
- **B — A, plus sums of several terms.** `1d8+1d6+3`, `2d6-1d4`, `1d12+1d12`. Consequence:
  covers weapon-plus-bonus-damage style rolls in one go. The expression reader becomes a small
  arithmetic parser rather than a pattern match, which is more work and more to get wrong, but it
  is a well-understood shape.
- **C — B, plus the common tabletop extras.** Keep-highest and keep-lowest (`4d6kh3`),
  advantage and disadvantage, exploding dice, multiplication. Consequence: this is where the work
  stops being small — each extra is its own rule with its own edge cases, and each one needs its
  own acceptance criteria and tests. It is a good tool at the end of it.
- **Recommendation:** A for now, with B and C available as further work once you have used it.
  You called it "a little command-line dice roller", and A is the reading of that which gets
  something in your hands soonest. If you know today that you will immediately want B, say so —
  building A then B costs more than building B.

## Answer

Option **A** — one dice term with an optional modifier — with the notation inside it delegated to
us. The stakeholder's words:

> [human] Just the usual — d20, 2d8+1, that kind of thing. Whatever's standard, don't overthink it.

This is read as an answer and not as a deferral, because it decides the thing the question was
about. Both expressions they named — `d20`, with neither a count nor a modifier, and `2d8+1`, with
both — are single dice terms, so neither reaches past option A; and *"don't overthink it"* is
evidence against option C, whose entire cost is the thinking. What their words leave open is the
notation *inside* "the usual", and that is a licence rather than a gap.

**Under delegation:** EP-001/Q-002 — the notation inside standard single-term dice expressions:
whether the count may be omitted, the sign and presence of the modifier, and the bounds on the
count, the die size and the modifier.

The delegation was spent in **ADR-0001**, which records the grammar
`[count]d<sides>[(+|-)modifier]`, its options and its reversibility, and which names the same
category on its own `**Under delegation:**` line. Two details were deliberately left inside the
licence and unspent — the letter case of `d`, and whitespace — because they are cheap to settle in
refinement and change nothing that is built.

What this answer does **not** license is reading "the usual" as option B. A sum of several terms
(`1d8+1d6+3`) is standard notation in some groups, and if it is standard in theirs then this
answer was read too narrowly. ADR-0001 `## Consequences` records what that would cost: additive
for the parser, but the breakdown line agreed in `WI-0001/Q-001` would have to be re-designed and
re-agreed. The place that puts it back in front of them is the sign-off, which names every answer
a delegation was spent under.
