## Context

`WI-0002`, round 2, question 2 of 3: what a date looks like when you write one. Question 1 was
about *where* the date goes on the line; this one is about *what you type in it*, and they are
separate decisions — any answer to one works with any answer to the other.

At `WI-0002/Q-001` you said a spend is dated today unless you say otherwise, and your reason was
about the monthly summary: *"a Saturday shop landing in the wrong month would make the monthly
summary wrong."* So the date has to be one the tool reads the same way you meant it, every time.
That is the whole of the risk here: a form the tool can read two ways is a form that will
eventually file a spend in the wrong month without telling you, which is exactly the failure
your answer was meant to prevent.

The case that decides it is a date like `7/9`. In the way dates are written in most of the
world that is the 7th of September; in the way they are written in the United States it is the
9th of July. The tool cannot tell which you meant, and neither can we — you have not told us
where you are, and it is not our business to guess it from anything else.

There is a second, smaller case: a day in the month just gone. If it is the 3rd of September and
you are catching up on August receipts, is `28` enough to mean the 28th of August, or would you
rather type the month out?

This question decides only how a date is *written*. Whether the tool refuses a date in the
future is question 3 of 3, and that is the last of them for this round.

## Question

Which ways of writing a date should the tool accept — and, if you want a short form like `7/9`,
which of the two readings of it is yours?

## Options considered

- **A — `2026-09-07` and nothing else.** The full year-month-day form, always ten characters.
  Consequence: it can only mean one thing, it sorts correctly, and it is the same form the tool
  already writes into its own file; nothing in it depends on where you live. The cost is that it
  is the longest of these to type, and it is the one form nobody says out loud.
- **B — `2026-09-07`, and `09-07` meaning that month and day of the current year.**
  Consequence: five fewer characters for the common case of a date in the last few weeks, still
  unambiguous, and `09-07` in December would still mean the 7th of September rather than
  something in December — which is either what you want or a trap, depending on how you catch up.
- **C — `2026-09-07`, and a short `7/9` meaning day-then-month.** Consequence: the fastest to
  type and the way you would say it; the cost is that the tool now has a rule about which number
  is which, and `7/9` and `9/7` are both valid dates that differ by two months.
- **D — `2026-09-07`, plus a plain-English `yesterday` and `last saturday`.** Consequence: the
  most natural to type for exactly the case you described — sitting down on Monday with the
  weekend's receipts. The cost is a lot more the tool has to understand, and more ways for it to
  read something as a date that you meant as something else.
- **Recommendation:** ours would be **A**, and we would revisit it once you have used the tool
  for a month. The whole value of the date is that the summary is right, and A is the only one of
  these where what the tool records cannot differ from what you meant. If ten characters turns
  out to be annoying in practice, adding a short form later breaks nothing that was already
  recorded — whereas guessing wrong about `7/9` now would quietly misfile spends from the start.
  Again, this is our preference and not a decision: if you want C, say so and say which reading
  is yours.

## Answer

[human] A — the full `2026-09-07` and nothing else. I don't want a short form; a spend in the
wrong month is exactly the thing I'm trying to get away from, and I'm only typing a date on the
odd occasion anyway. For the record, if you ever do add one, `7/9` means the 7th of September to
me.
