## Context

Money does not divide evenly. A £10.00 taxi shared by three people is £3.333… each. Whatever the
tool does with the third of a penny has to be stated, because WI-0003's AC2 asks a reader to
reproduce the report's amounts by hand — and they cannot do that unless the rule is written
down. This is a fact about your preference, not something intake can read out of the idea.

The same choice also decides whether the tool works in whole pence internally or in decimal
amounts, which is hard to change later once data has been written to disk.

## Question

When an expense does not divide evenly among the people sharing it, what should happen to the
leftover pennies?

## Options considered

- **A — Round to the nearest penny and give the remainder to the payer.** £10 split three ways:
  each sharer owes £3.33, the payer absorbs the extra penny. Consequence: totals always balance
  exactly; the person who paid is very slightly out of pocket, which is usually invisible and
  occasionally annoying.
- **B — Round to the nearest penny and spread the remainder deterministically** — e.g. give the
  extra pennies to the first sharers in alphabetical order, so £10 three ways is £3.34, £3.33,
  £3.33. Consequence: fairest over many expenses and still exactly reproducible by hand;
  slightly more to explain in the report's documentation.
- **C — Keep fractions and print more decimal places.** Consequence: nobody can pay £3.333, so
  the rounding just moves to the moment of settling; rejected unless you want it.
- **Recommendation:** B. It keeps the totals balancing (WI-0003 AC3), it is reproducible by a
  reader with a calculator (AC2), and it does not quietly penalise whoever was generous enough
  to pay.

**The stakeholder declined to choose and authorised proceeding without them, so the architect
decided.** Route: decided, recorded as
`docs/architecture/adr/ADR-0001-money-in-integer-pence-with-largest-remainder-split.md`.
`answered-by` is `answer-questions`, not `human`, because the substance of the answer below is
the architect's decision and not the stakeholder's — the stakeholder supplied only the
authorisation to make it.

This was not re-escalated. None of the four conditions in `spec/question.md` §4 applies to a
question the stakeholder has been shown, has read, and has explicitly handed back; re-asking it
would spend the scarcest thing in the loop on a choice they just declined to make.

**The decision — option B, in whole pence.** For an expense of `A` pence shared by `n` people:
`base = A // n`, `r = A % n`; sort the sharers by name case-insensitively; the first `r` of them
owe `base + 1` pence, the rest owe `base`. £10.00 shared by Ana, Ben and Cass is £3.34, £3.33,
£3.33. Money is held as an integer number of pence throughout, including in the stored data; no
floating-point value ever holds an amount.

Why B over the alternatives, in the terms WI-0003's criteria are written in: it keeps AC3's
balance exact by construction (the shares sum to the amount, so the balances sum to zero), it is
reproducible by a reader with a calculator as AC2 demands, and unlike option A it does not
quietly charge the person who paid. Option C fails AC3 as written and option D — floats — fails
it intermittently, which is worse.

ADR-0001 records the reversibility, which matters here: the split rule is one function and is
cheap to change, but the whole-pence storage is expensive to reverse once real data exists. If
the stakeholder does come back with a preference, it supersedes ADR-0001 rather than amending
it.
