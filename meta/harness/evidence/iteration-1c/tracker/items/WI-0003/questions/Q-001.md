## Context

"Show who owes whom" has two common and materially different meanings, and intake will not pick
one for you because they produce different output from the same data and the acceptance criteria
for WI-0003 have to say which is right.

Worked example. Ana, Ben and Cass. Ana pays £30 for dinner shared by all three. Ben pays £15 for
taxis shared by all three. Cass pays nothing.

- Read one, every debt as it arose: Ben owes Ana £10, Cass owes Ana £10, Ana owes Ben £5, Cass
  owes Ben £5. Four statements, two of which cancel each other partly out.
- Read two, the smallest set of transfers that settles everyone: Cass owes Ana £10, Cass owes
  Ben £5, Ben owes Ana £5 — or, netted further, Cass pays Ana £15 and Ben pays Ana... the point
  is that a settlement algorithm produces fewer, larger transfers and the individual expenses are
  no longer visible in them.

Both are correct arithmetic. They are answers to different questions.

## Question

Should the report list every pairwise debt as it arose from the individual expenses, or the
smallest set of payments that would settle the whole group?

## Options considered

- **A — Pairwise debts, netted per pair.** For each pair of people, one line with the net amount
  between just those two. In the example: Ben owes Ana £5, Cass owes Ana £10, Cass owes Ben £5.
  Consequence: every line is traceable back to expenses those two people were both involved in,
  so anyone can check it; the number of lines grows with the square of the group size.
- **B — Minimal settlement.** Compute each person's overall balance, then emit the fewest
  transfers that zero them all. Consequence: fewest payments to actually make, which is what a
  group settling up wants; the transfers do not correspond to any particular expense, so "why do
  I owe Cass, I never ate with Cass?" has no answer the tool can give.
- **C — Both, in one report.** Balances per person first, then the suggested transfers.
  Consequence: most useful and most checkable; slightly more work in WI-0003 and a longer output.
- **Recommendation:** C. The per-person balance is what makes the report auditable (and is what
  AC3 checks), and the suggested transfers are what makes it actionable. Building B alone is a
  false economy because the balances have to be computed for it anyway.

## Answer

[human] I just want the actual payments — who pays whom to settle up. Don't need every individual debt listed out.

**Read by `answer-questions` as option B — minimal settlement.** The stakeholder rejected the
recommendation (C, balances plus transfers) and named the transfers alone: "the actual payments
— who pays whom to settle up", with the pairwise reading explicitly declined ("Don't need every
individual debt listed out"). Route: recorded intent — the stakeholder's own words, not a
derivation, so no ADR was written for it.

What this fixes, and what it deliberately does not:

- The report prints payments, each "X pays Y an amount", and making exactly those payments
  leaves everyone square. It is not required to print one line per pair of people, and a printed
  payment need not correspond to any single expense.
- Per-person balances are still computed internally — a settlement cannot be derived without
  them — but printing them is not required by any criterion. The stakeholder did not ask for it,
  and `answer-questions` will not add a requirement they did not state. Whether to print them as
  context is a presentation choice left to `refine`.
- AC1 gained an upper bound on the number of payments (at most one fewer than the number of
  people with a non-zero balance). Without it "minimal" is not decidable by observation, and
  every listing of pairwise debts would technically also settle the group. The bound is the
  property a greedy settlement always achieves, so it does not commit `plan` to an algorithm.
