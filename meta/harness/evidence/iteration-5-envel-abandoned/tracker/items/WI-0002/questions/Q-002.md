## Context

WI-0002, refinement round 1, question 2 of 3 — the same conversation as Q-001, about the item that
adds income, spending and balances.

Every command this item adds takes an amount, and nothing in the record says what an amount is
allowed to look like when you type it. That sounds like a detail and it is not, because it decides
what happens on an ordinary Tuesday when you type the thing you would naturally type. Three
separate decisions hide in it, and they are all about your money rather than about our code:

- **The symbol.** If you habitually type `£12.50`, then a tool that only accepts `12.50` refuses
  you several times a day until you retrain yourself.
- **The pence.** `12` and `12.50` are both obvious. `12.5` is probably twelve pounds fifty.
- **More precision than money has.** `12.345` is not an amount of money. The tool can refuse it,
  or it can round it to `12.35` and carry on — and rounding means the number it stores is not the
  number you typed. You told us, about overspending, "the whole point is that the number is the
  truth", which is why we are not deciding this one on our own.

We are not asking about the output — what the balance looks like when it is printed back is ours
to make consistent. This is only about what you are allowed to type.

## Question

What should an amount look like when you type it — is a currency symbol accepted, and what should
the tool do when you type more decimal places than money has, such as `12.345`?

## Options considered

- **A — plain numbers only, and more than two decimals is refused.** `12`, `12.5` and `12.50` are
  accepted; `£12.50` is refused; `12.345` is refused with a message saying an amount has at most
  two decimal places. Consequence: nothing is ever stored that you did not type, which is the
  "number is the truth" rule applied to precision as well as to overspending. Cost: a refusal on
  the day you paste something with a `£` in front of it, and a refusal on a genuine third decimal
  — a per-unit price, a foreign receipt — rather than a helpful rounding.
- **B — plain numbers only, and more than two decimals is rounded** to the nearest penny and
  recorded. Consequence: nothing is ever refused for being too precise, and you never have to
  retype. Cost: the stored number is not the typed number, silently. Over a month of receipts the
  balance drifts from the arithmetic you would do by hand, and the tool stops being able to say
  where the difference came from.
- **C — a leading `£` is accepted and ignored, otherwise as A.** `£12.50` and `12.50` are the same
  amount; `12.345` is still refused. Consequence: the thing you type either way works. Cost: one
  more shape to accept, and a decision to revisit if you ever budget in something that is not
  pounds — this item has no notion of a second currency at all.
- **Recommendation (ours, not yours):** **C**. Refusing over-precision rather than rounding it is
  the same rule you already chose for overspending, and accepting a `£` you might type costs
  nothing and prevents a daily annoyance. If you never type a `£`, C and A are the same tool.

## Answer

[human] C. Take a `£` if I type one and ignore it, and refuse anything with more than two
decimals rather than rounding it — same reason as the overspend, I don't want the tool storing a
number I didn't type. It's one currency, pounds and pence, and that's not changing.
